Overview

Two photographs taken from the same spot, with the camera turned between them, are related by a single 3 by 3 matrix. The relationship is exact and it holds right across the frame. If the centre of projection does not move, every point in one image maps to a point in the other through one projective transform, and recovering that matrix is enough to lay both pictures down in the same plane.

That is the whole of Part A. Shoot overlapping frames, pick corresponding points by hand, solve for the matrix that relates them, warp one image into the other's frame, and blend the overlap so the join does not show. Part B, further down this page, replaces the hand picked points with corner detection and automatic matching, and changes nothing else, so everything in Part A is the scaffolding the automatic version runs on.

The constraint the handout puts on this is that the warping and the homography solve have to be written from scratch. No library that takes two point sets and hands back a warped image. That is the part worth doing, so the sections below spend their time on the solve, the inverse warp and the resampling. Part B is written from scratch on the same terms, with one exception the assignment names: the Harris response itself comes from the provided harris.py, which the handout says to use rather than reimplement.

A.1 · Shoot the pictures

The one rule that matters when shooting is that the camera rotates and does not travel. A homography only describes the relationship between two views if they share a centre of projection, so turning on the spot is fine and taking a step sideways is not. Anything with depth in it will give the mistake away: near and far objects shift by different amounts, no single matrix can satisfy both, and the overlap ends up with doubled edges that no amount of blending will hide.

A.2 · Recover homographies

A homography maps homogeneous coordinates through a 3 by 3 matrix, and the result is divided by its own third component to come back to image coordinates. Scaling the whole matrix changes nothing, so there are eight free parameters and not nine. Fixing h33 = 1 is the usual way to pin the scale down, and it turns the problem into a linear solve.

Writing out one correspondence, a point (x, y) in the first image landing on (x', y') in the second: 

x' = (h11*x + h12*y + h13) / (h31*x + h32*y + 1)
y' = (h21*x + h22*y + h23) / (h31*x + h32*y + 1)

Those are nonlinear in the unknowns only because of the division. Multiplying both sides by the denominator clears it and leaves two equations that are linear in the eight unknowns: 

h11*x + h12*y + h13 - h31*x*x' - h32*y*x' = x'
h21*x + h22*y + h23 - h31*x*y' - h32*y*y' = y'

So every correspondence contributes two rows. Stacking n of them gives a 2n by 8 system: 

A @ h = b        h = [h11 h12 h13 h21 h22 h23 h31 h32].T

per point, the two rows of A and the two entries of b:

  [ x  y  1  0  0  0  -x*x'  -y*x' ]        [ x' ]
  [ 0  0  0  x  y  1  -x*y'  -y*y' ]        [ y' ]

Four points give exactly eight equations and a unique solution, which also means any error in picking those four goes straight into the matrix with nothing to push back on it. Using more than four makes the system overdetermined and there is generally no exact solution, so it gets solved in the least squares sense instead. The extra points average the clicking error out, and the residual becomes a usable signal: a correspondence I misplaced shows up as one pair of rows with a much larger reprojection error than the rest.

The correspondences

Every pair here uses four correspondences, which is the minimum and a real shortfall. Four points determine the homography exactly, so the solve reproduces them perfectly and the reprojection error comes out as 0.00 px for all five pairs. That number is arithmetic, not a score. With more than four the fit has to compromise, the residuals stop being zero, and a point I had misplaced would stand out as one large residual among small ones.

What the four points sit on turned out to matter more than how many of them there were. My first attempt put all four on the corners of one small sign, covering under one per cent of the frame. The cafe pair failed outright: warping its left frame into the right left a mean intensity difference near 40 out of 255 across the overlap, and the frame ballooned to ten times its own area.

Spreading the same four points to cover about a third of the frame cut that difference by nearly half and the mosaic came together. Same count, same photographs, same code.

The reason is conditioning. I jittered every clicked point by one pixel and watched where the warped frame corners moved to. Bunched, a one pixel error shifted a corner by more than a thousand pixels. Spread wide, by under a hundred. Four points in a tight cluster pin the transform down near that cluster and leave it almost unconstrained everywhere else, so the fit extrapolates on faith.

The points themselves went on corners of rigid things: door frames, notice boards, the edges of the mailbox grids, the line where skirting meets floor. Nothing on a reflection, because a specular highlight sits in a different place for every viewpoint and would pull the solve toward a correspondence that is not one.

A.3 · Warp the images

The warp runs backwards. Stepping through the source image and pushing each pixel to where the homography sends it leaves the output full of holes, because the mapping stretches some regions and no integer source pixel lands in the gaps. Stepping through the output instead, and asking where each destination pixel came from, gives every output pixel exactly one answer. That needs H inverse, and it needs a value at a fractional source coordinate, which is where the interpolation comes in.

The output canvas is sized before any of that happens. Warping the four corners of the source through H and taking the extremes gives the bounding box of the result, which is where the translation that keeps everything at positive coordinates comes from. The handout's convention is that pixel (0, 0) is the centre of the first pixel rather than its corner, and getting that wrong shifts everything by half a pixel, which is invisible in a single warp and shows up as a soft doubled edge once two frames are stacked. 

corners = [[0, 0], [w - 1, 0], [w - 1, h - 1], [0, h - 1]]
warped  = H @ corners_homogeneous        # then divide through by row 3
x0, y0  = floor(warped.min(axis=1))      # bounding box of the output
x1, y1  = ceil(warped.max(axis=1))

for each output pixel p in that box:
    src = H_inv @ (p + [x0, y0])         # back to source coordinates
    out[p] = sample(im, src)             # nearest neighbour, or bilinear

Pixels whose source coordinate falls outside the input get no value at all, so the warp carries an alpha mask alongside the colour. That mask is what the blending step in A.4 uses to tell a genuine black pixel from a region that was never covered.

The two interpolators differ only in sample. Nearest neighbour rounds the source coordinate and reads one pixel. Bilinear takes the four pixels around it and mixes them by how close each one is, which costs four reads and a handful of multiplies per output pixel instead of one read.

Graphics teaches the same operation as three lerps, and that is how I wrote it. Interpolate along the top edge between the two upper pixels, interpolate along the bottom edge between the two lower ones, then interpolate between those two results. The blend factors are just the fractional parts of the source coordinate. Writing it that way makes two things obvious that the weighted-sum version hides: a sample landing exactly on a pixel centre has both fractions at zero and collapses to a single read, and because the operation is separable the order does not matter, so interpolating down the columns first and across afterwards gives the same answer.

Rectification

Rectification is the single-image test of all of this. Photograph something known to be rectangular from an angle, pick its four corners, and map them onto an actual rectangle. The correspondences are invented rather than matched, which means the result is checkable by eye: if the solve and the warp are right, the thing comes out square on, and if the matrix is subtly wrong the rectangle comes out as a trapezium.

Nearest neighbour against bilinear

Nearest neighbour took 211 ms and bilinear 456 ms on the same warp, so bilinear costs about 2.2 times as much. Both are a 1200 pixel frame going into an 809 by 1605 output.

On average the two results differ by 2.04 in intensity out of 255, which sounds like nothing. The average is misleading. All of the disagreement is on edges and the flat regions drag the mean down; the worst single pixel differs by 112.

The two crops above are the same 256 pixel window from each. The blind slats are a stack of high contrast near-horizontal edges, which is the worst case for nearest neighbour, and it shows: every slat edge has visible stair stepping where it crosses a pixel row. Bilinear is smooth along the same edges and slightly softer everywhere else.

At this resolution bilinear is worth the extra 245 ms without needing an argument. The warps finish in under half a second and there are only a handful of them. The sums would look different on full size frames, where one warp is closer to half a minute, or on video where it runs per frame.

The gap also grows with how hard the warp stretches. Where a region is magnified, many output pixels fall between the same few source pixels: nearest neighbour gives them all one value and produces visible blocks, while bilinear keeps a gradient across them. That is why I used the blinds rectification for this comparison instead of one of the mosaic warps, since it magnifies far more in one corner.

A.4 · Blend into a mosaic

With a homography and a warp, the mosaic is mostly bookkeeping. Pick one frame to be the reference and leave it alone, warp the others into its plane, work out a canvas big enough for all of them, and composite. For three frames the middle one is the natural reference, because warping the two outer frames inward distorts each of them less than chaining both onto one end would.

Compositing is where the choice is. Overwriting one image with the other gives a visible line wherever the two disagree, and they always disagree a little: exposure drifts between frames, vignetting darkens the edges, and the homography is never exact, so the overlap is misregistered by a fraction of a pixel. A weighted average across the overlap spreads that disagreement out instead of concentrating it on one line. The usual weight is a distance transform, high in the middle of each frame and falling to zero at its edge, so each pixel is dominated by whichever image had it furthest from a border.

A weighted average has its own failure, which is that it blurs anything the two frames do not agree on geometrically. A Laplacian pyramid blend fixes that by blending each frequency band over a different width: wide for the low frequencies, where a slow ramp hides exposure differences, and narrow for the high frequencies, where a wide ramp would smear detail. That is the same multiresolution blending as project 2, applied to a seam instead of an apple and an orange.

What the blending buys

All three use the two band blend. Each frame is split into a blurred version and the detail that is left over. The blurred part cross fades slowly across the overlap, which is what hides the exposure difference between frames. The detail is cut hard at the seam, with no averaging anywhere.

That hard cut is the part that matters, and I had it backwards at first. My first version faded every band across the whole overlap, the finest one included. Averaging two copies of an edge that sit a few pixels apart draws both of them at half strength, which is exactly what a ghost is. The corridor mosaic had a visibly doubled doorway until the detail band was cut instead of faded. Nothing else changed, including the points.

The seam lands in the middle of the overlap on its own. Each pixel goes to whichever frame holds it furthest from that frame's own border, and those two distances cross over halfway.

Three different things make a join visible

Blending only helps with one of them. An exposure step it hides, by fading the low frequencies over a few hundred pixels. Misregistration it cannot fix, and the most it can do is refuse to average it into a ghost. Parallax from the camera having moved it cannot touch at all, because no single matrix was ever going to align the scene.

Those first two are worth separating before judging anything, and my first attempt at measuring did not. Taking the mean absolute difference across an overlap mixes a brightness difference together with a failure to line up, and the two want completely different fixes. Running a high pass over both frames first removes the exposure part, since a brightness difference is almost entirely low frequency, and what survives is the structural error.

Mosaic 3 is the opposite: the smallest exposure difference of any set and the largest structural error.

The structure column still will not rank the three against each other, which took me embarrassingly long to notice. It is an intensity difference, so it depends on how much detail the scene holds as much as on how well the frames line up. The corridor is mostly blank painted wall, around a fifth the local detail of the mailroom, and one of its joins has barely a handful of textured patches anywhere in the overlap. A scene with nothing in it scores well for free.

The measure that does not care what is in the scene is how far a patch has to move to line up. In those terms the mailroom's left join is the tightest of the five and the corridor's left join is the loosest, which is close to the reverse of what the structure column suggests. So the honest summary is that each of these numbers answers a different question, and none of them is the ranking.

Picking the points out of different parts of the wall made the trade obvious. Choosing correspondences on the small things, the box numbers and the label holders, lined those up and pushed the mailbox frames out of register. Choosing them on the frames and the panelling squared the frames up and left every label doubled. There was no choice of four points that did both.

Two surfaces at slightly different depths explains it, and a mailbox bank is exactly that: the doors sit recessed inside their wooden surrounds by a few centimetres. That is enough for a small sideways drift between frames to need one alignment for the doors and a different one for the frames around them. The block shifts back it up, scattering between minus eight and plus eight pixels across the overlap instead of agreeing on one number.

So the mailroom is a plane at arm's length and two planes up close, and which one you get depends on where you put the points. A flat wall is only flat at the scale you look at it.

The stretch on mosaic 1

Separately from any of that, mosaic 1 stretches hard on the left, and that is the 43 degrees from A.1 arriving. On a flat canvas the outer frame has to grow without limit as the angle opens, and 43 degrees is far enough for it to dominate the picture. The standard fix is to project onto a cylinder instead of a plane, which turns the camera's rotation into a slide along the surface so the frames no longer have to stretch to meet. That is not something this part does. The stretch is a property of the canvas, and it says nothing about how well the two frames line up, which is a separate question.

What Part A taught me

The shared centre of projection is a constraint on where you stand, not a detail of the algebra, and you can measure afterwards whether you honoured it. Factoring each homography back into a camera matrix and a rotation gave residuals under 0.015 on four of my five pairs. That says more about how I shot the set than any reprojection error I could quote, and it caught the one pair where I probably drifted.

Where the points go beats how many there are, at least at the margin I was working in. Spreading four points from one per cent of the frame to most of it cut how far a frame corner travels per pixel of clicking error by more than ten times, and turned a mosaic that failed into one that works. Count alone does not rescue a tight cluster: twenty points bunched on one sign are worse conditioned than four points spread wide, by around a factor of twenty five. I would still rather have twelve, because four of anything gives a reprojection error of zero and no way to catch my own mistakes.

Inverse warping is forced, not preferred. Pushing source pixels forward scatters them to fractional positions and leaves holes wherever the transform stretches. Walking the output and asking where each pixel came from gives every one of them exactly one answer, which is why the code needs the inverse matrix and an interpolator and could not be written the other way round.

Blending and averaging are different operations, and project 2 did not teach me that because its two images were already aligned. Here they are not. Fade the fine detail across an overlap and a misalignment becomes a ghost; cut it and the same misalignment becomes a seam you can put somewhere unobtrusive. Which band gets faded and which gets cut is the whole decision.

Measure the right thing or the measurement will mislead you, and I got this wrong twice on the same page. First I judged these mosaics by the mean difference across their overlaps and called the cafe pair my worst registered, when nearly all of that number was an exposure step, the one defect the blending already handles. Then I split the exposure out and trusted what was left, which rewards a scene for being empty: the corridor scored best largely because it is blank wall. Both numbers were correct about what they measure and neither was measuring what I was asking.

Flat is a question about scale. The mailroom is one wall, so a homography should fit it exactly no matter where I stood, and that was the reason I expected it to be the easy set. Up close it is two surfaces: mailbox doors recessed a few centimetres inside their wooden surrounds. That is plenty for one alignment to suit the doors and a different one to suit the frames, and no four points can satisfy both at once.

Part B · Autostitching

Everything above rests on four points per pair that I typed in by hand. Part B removes that step and leaves the rest alone. The solver, the inverse warp, the canvas sizing and the two band blend are the same functions running on the same photographs, so the mosaics at the end of this page sit directly against the ones in A.4 and the only thing that differs between them is where the correspondences came from.

The method is the one in Brown, Szeliski and Winder, Multi-Image Matching using Multi-Scale Oriented Patches, with the simplifications the handout allows: one scale, axis aligned patches, no wavelet indexing and no sub pixel refinement. It runs as a funnel, and watching the counts fall is the shortest description of what each stage is for. 

Harris, every local maximum           about 20,000   per frame
  trimmed by strength                        3,000   per frame
  adaptive non maximal suppression             500   per frame
  the ratio test                          33 to 99   matches, per join
  RANSAC                                  19 to 75   inliers, per join

Every stage throws away far more than it keeps and each one throws away a different kind of thing. Suppression discards corners that are real but crowded. The ratio test discards corners that are real and well spread but have no counterpart in the other frame. RANSAC discards pairs that look alike and are in the wrong place. Only the last of those uses geometry at all, which is why it comes last.

B.5, the bells and whistles, is optional for CS180 and I did not attempt it.

B.1 · Detect the corners

The Harris response measures how much a patch of the image changes under a small shift, in every direction at once. A flat patch changes under no shift, an edge changes under a shift across it and not one along it, and a corner changes under both. The handout supplies harris.py and says to use it rather than reimplement the detector, so the response and its local maxima are the one part of Part B I did not write.

What it comes with is a coordinate convention trap. harris.py returns its corners as (row, column) and every function in Part A takes (x, y). The two agree on a square image and disagree silently everywhere else, which is the kind of bug that shows up three stages later as nothing matching and no clue why. I convert once, on the way out of the detector, and the test for it puts a single corner at x = 90, y = 60 so that a swap cannot pass by symmetry.

Around twenty thousand corners come back per frame, which at this working resolution is one for every forty pixels of image area. I keep the strongest three thousand before going further, because the next step compares every candidate against every stronger candidate and that cost grows with the square of the count.

Adaptive non maximal suppression

Keeping the five hundred strongest corners is the obvious way down to a workable number and it is the wrong one. Corner strength is a measure of local contrast, so the strongest corners pile up wherever the scene has the most contrast, which in my cafe frame is one lit merchandise case covering about a fifth of the picture. A.2 already showed what that costs. Four points clustered on one per cent of the frame moved a warped corner by over a thousand pixels per pixel of clicking error, and the same four spread wide moved it by under a hundred. A homography wants its evidence spread out.

Section 3 of the paper gets the spread without abandoning strength. Give every corner a suppression radius, defined as the distance to the nearest corner that is meaningfully stronger than it is: 

r_i = min ||x_i - x_j||     over every j with  f(x_i) < c_robust * f(x_j)

c_robust = 0.9, so a neighbour has to be about 1.11 times as strong to count

Then keep the five hundred largest radii. A corner survives if nothing stronger sits near it, which a genuinely strong corner achieves by being strong and a modest corner achieves by being the best thing in its own neighbourhood. The global maximum has no stronger neighbour anywhere, so its radius is infinite and it is always kept.

The implementation is a sort and a prefix. With the corners in descending order of strength, the points that can suppress corner i are exactly those with f(x_j) > f(x_i) / 0.9, and because the list is sorted those form a prefix of it. One searchsorted call finds where each prefix ends, so corner i is measured against that prefix instead of against all three thousand.

Keeping the strongest five hundred puts them about six pixels apart. Suppression roughly doubles or triples that, to around fifteen to seventeen. The suppression radius is also a floor rather than an average: the smallest radius kept on the cafe frame is 17.7 pixels, so every corner in that selection is at least that far from anything stronger than it.

B.2 · Describe them

A corner's position is useless on its own. Matching a corner in one frame against a corner in the other needs a description of what the image looks like around it, short enough to compare a quarter of a million times per join and stable enough to survive everything that changes between two photographs.

Section 4 of the paper takes a 40 by 40 window around the corner, blurs it, samples every fifth pixel to give an 8 by 8 patch, and normalises that patch to zero mean and unit deviation. Sixty four numbers. Three things are happening and each buys a different kind of tolerance.

Sampling coarsely over a wide window buys tolerance to the detector missing the corner. A two pixel slip moves an 8 by 8 patch by a quarter of its own width, which changes it into a different patch, and moves a 40 by 40 window by a twentieth, which barely changes it at all. The test measures exactly this. Under a deliberate two pixel slip the descriptor moves by 30.5 in squared distance where a raw 8 by 8 crop of the same image moves by 110.3.

Blurring first is what makes the coarse sampling legitimate. Taking every fifth pixel can only represent detail coarser than about ten pixels across. Finer detail does not disappear, it aliases into a false pattern, and which false pattern you get depends on where the sample grid happened to land. That is the position sensitivity the wide window was supposed to remove, arriving again through the back door. The sigma of 2.0 is the anti aliasing width skimage applies for a downscale of five, which is the same operation.

Normalising buys tolerance to exposure. If the second frame is brighter and flatter the patch becomes a*d + b, its mean becomes a*mean + b and its deviation becomes a*deviation, so subtracting the mean and dividing by the deviation cancels both constants exactly. That matters on these photographs in particular. A.4 found that almost the whole disagreement along the cafe join is a brightness step between the frames, and without this the descriptors would be comparing exposure as much as structure.

The patches read as structure rather than noise, which is the sign that the blur and the sampling rate are in balance. Bars, steps and corners at various angles are legible in them, and the mid grey is the zero that the normalisation put there.

An epsilon that broke the thing it was guarding

The normalisation divides by a standard deviation, which is zero on a perfectly flat patch, so it needs a guard. I wrote the usual one, dividing by std + 1e-8, and a test caught it straight away. Bias and gain were supposed to cancel to floating point and they cancelled only to about 1e-6.

The error grew as the image got dimmer: 2.3e-6 at a gain of 0.5, 2.1e-5 at 0.1, 2.3e-4 at 0.01. That scaling is the entire diagnosis. An added constant is absolute and the deviation it is added to is not, so a patch and a dimmer copy of that patch get perturbed by different relative amounts and the gain stops cancelling. The guard was destroying the invariance it was sitting inside.

A floor rather than an addition fixes it. max(std, 1e-12) leaves every real patch untouched and engages only on a patch with no contrast at all, where there is nothing left to preserve. Bias and gain now cancel to around 1e-13.

B.3 · Match them

Five hundred descriptors on each side make a quarter of a million possible pairings per join, and the correct ones number in the tens. Nearest neighbour gets most of the way there, with one problem: it always returns something. A corner that is not in the overlapping region at all still has a nearest neighbour, and that match is wrong by construction.

The obvious filter is to drop matches whose distance is above some threshold, and it does not work. How close a wrong match gets depends on the feature. A corner on a detailed, unusual object has no near twin anywhere and its wrong matches are all far away. A corner on a plain painted wall finds something nearly identical in any frame at all. One threshold cannot serve both.

Lowe's ratio test asks the better question. Rather than asking whether the best match is good, it asks whether the best match is much better than the second best: 

ratio = squared distance to the nearest / squared distance to the second nearest
keep the match when ratio < 0.64

The second nearest neighbour estimates how close a wrong match gets for this particular feature, so dividing by it calibrates the bar per feature instead of globally. A corner with no partner in the other frame has two unrelated neighbours at similar distances and a ratio near one. The same test catches repeated structure, where the best and the second best are both excellent and choosing between them is a coin toss, which describes the mailroom's wall of identical mailboxes exactly.

The threshold comes from figure 6b of the paper, which plots this ratio for correct and incorrect matches and is drawn on squared errors. Lowe's familiar 0.8 is the same threshold quoted on distances, and 0.8 squared is 0.64.

I also require the match to be mutual, meaning each descriptor has to be the other's nearest neighbour. The handout does not ask for it, and it costs nothing because the full distance matrix already exists. It removes the case of several features all latching onto one bland descriptor.

Green is what RANSAC went on to accept and red is what it rejected, which makes these figures a picture of what the ratio test cannot do. Every red line joins a pair of corners that genuinely look alike. The test filters on appearance and nothing in it knows where anything is, so a pair that looks right and sits in the wrong place passes. Separating those out takes geometry.

B.4 · RANSAC, and the automatic mosaics

Between 23 and 42 per cent of the matches that survive the ratio test are wrong, and handing those to the least squares solver from A.2 does not work at all. Least squares minimises a sum of squared errors, so its answer is dominated by the largest terms. A match that is three hundred pixels wrong contributes ninety thousand to that sum where a correct match contributes one, so a single bad match can move the answer arbitrarily far. On planted test data the difference is a fit that lands 0.29 pixels from the truth against one that lands 785 pixels from it.

RANSAC turns the problem around. Rather than fitting to everything and hoping the errors cancel, fit to the smallest number of points that determine an answer at all, then let the rest of the data vote on whether that answer was any good. 

1. draw 4 matches at random
2. solve for H exactly, which is A.2's solver on a square 8 by 8 system
3. count the matches whose own mapping agrees with H to within 3 px
4. keep the largest such set, the consensus
5. refit H by least squares on that consensus alone

Four is the minimal sample because a homography has eight degrees of freedom and each correspondence supplies two equations. Minimal is what you want, for a reason that is easy to miss: a draw is clean with probability w to the power of the sample size, so every extra point the model needs makes a clean draw exponentially rarer.

Step 5 is the one that is easy to skip and should not be. Four points determine the homography exactly, which means they reproduce their own measurement noise exactly too, and they do it worst far from where they sit. Refitting on the whole consensus averages that noise out. On the planted data it is the difference between 307 pixels of error and 0.29.

It is also the first time Part A's overdetermined path does any work. Every clicked join used exactly four points, so A.2's reprojection error of 0.00 px was arithmetic rather than a measurement. Here the refit runs on between nineteen and seventy five inliers, and the residual it reports is a real number: 0.77 to 1.19 pixels mean across the five joins.

How many draws are enough

If a fraction w of the matches are inliers then a draw of four is clean with probability w to the fourth, so the number of draws that gives at least one clean sample with probability p is 

N = log(1 - p) / log(1 - w^4)        p = 0.99

w is not known in advance, so I estimate it from the best consensus so far and recompute the budget whenever that improves. It starts at the 2000 cap and collapses as soon as the loop finds anything decent. The runs took between fifteen and forty draws, which follows from inlier fractions between 58 and 77 per cent: at those rates somewhere between one draw in three and one in nine is clean.

The worst residuals on the two mailroom joins, 3.08 and 3.10 pixels, sit just over the three pixel tolerance. That is not an inconsistency. A match is admitted on its distance under one homography and then reported on its distance under the refitted one, and the refit shifts slightly to accommodate everybody it admitted.

The refit also runs more than once. A clean sample of four is not necessarily a good one: four correct matches that happen to sit close together pin the homography down near themselves and extrapolate badly, so they can reject matches that are perfectly correct. A test with twenty planted inliers caught this, finding only seventeen of them. Rescoring with the refitted homography and refitting again while the consensus keeps growing found all twenty, and on the real photographs it added inliers to four of the five joins: 27 to 29, 68 to 75, 45 to 53 and 34 to 36, with the corridor's left join already settled at 19. Three passes was the most any join needed, and the counts are in the table above.

That is not the write up I expected. Where the automatic version pulls ahead is the mailroom, and the mailroom is the set whose problem was never the points.

Two matrices that disagree and align the same

The first comparison I ran was between the matrices themselves, and it said something completely different. Taking a grid of points across the source frame and measuring how far apart the two homographies send them, the cafe join's two matrices disagree by 92 pixels on average and 873 at worst. Those are not small numbers, and the overlap measurement above has the same two matrices registering that join to within two intensity levels of each other.

Inside the overlap the two agree to under two and a half pixels on four joins of five. All the rest is extrapolation into the part of the frame that has no counterpart in the other photograph, where neither method had any evidence at all and the matrix is only continuing the shape the points it did have gave it. A homography is pinned down where the correspondences are and free everywhere else.

That is also why the canvases come out different sizes. The cafe mosaic is 5011 by 2897 pixels when clicked and 4246 by 2368 automatically, nearly a third smaller in area, because the canvas is sized from where the warped corners land and the corners are precisely the part being extrapolated.

So comparing two homographies entry by entry, or by how far apart they send arbitrary points, mostly measures how they extrapolate. The question worth asking is how well each registers the pixels that are in both frames.

The mailroom is still the mailroom

Automating the correspondences did not rescue the set that failed in A.4, and it was never going to. Both mailroom joins stay the worst of the five under either method, at 11.70 and 11.99 structural against 2.42 for the best join on the page. A.4 traced that to two surfaces at different depths, mailbox doors recessed a few centimetres inside their wooden surrounds, and no single homography fits two planes at once.

RANSAC cannot help, because what it is good at is not what is wrong here. It finds the largest set of matches consistent with one homography. Where the scene offers two mutually inconsistent sets, it keeps the larger and discards the other as outliers, which is a correct answer to the question RANSAC asks and still the wrong model for the scene. Better correspondences did help the right join, taking it from 15.20 to 11.99, and left the left join where it already was at 11.70 against 11.61. Neither comes near the 2.42 the corridor manages, and nothing in Part B was going to close that gap, because the gap is the wall.

What Part B taught me

Automating the correspondences changed the result far less than I expected it to. I had written A.2's admission that four points per pair is a shortfall assuming Part B would show me what that shortfall cost, and on four of the five joins it showed me nothing: the two homographies register their overlap within two intensity levels of each other, out of 255. Four points chosen slowly on rigid corners are accurate points, and accurate points leave little clicking error for tens of inliers to average out. The honest summary is that RANSAC matched my clicking rather than beat it, which is still the result worth having, because it matched it without me clicking anything.

The one exception says where the limit actually sits. The only join that moved was the mailroom's right one, 15.20 down to 11.99, and after moving it is still the worst join on this page, and still five times the corridor's 2.42. Three levels of improvement on a join that far out is the method being beside the point rather than the method working. Whatever holds these mosaics back, it is not how I picked the points.

Spread is the same lesson twice. A.2 found four points bunched on one sign conditioning the solve catastrophically worse than four spread across the frame. ANMS is that finding turned into an algorithm, and the paper arrives at it from the same direction: keeping the strongest corners gets you a crowd, and a crowd constrains the transform where it already is and nowhere else.

Ask whether the best match is unusually good, not whether it is good. The ratio test is one division and it replaces a threshold that cannot work with one that can, because dividing by the second nearest neighbour measures the match against how easy matching is for that particular feature. It is the cheapest idea in the paper and it does the most work.

Two matrices can disagree by a hundred pixels and align the same photographs identically, because the disagreement lives where neither of them had any evidence. I nearly wrote the automatic pipeline off on the strength of that first number. This is the third time on this page that the measurement I reached for first was answering a different question from the one I was asking, and the pattern in all three is the same: measure the thing you actually care about, which here was the pixels in the overlap.

A clean minimal sample is not the same as a good one. Four correct matches sitting close together give a homography that is right where they are and wrong elsewhere, so it rejects matches that are perfectly fine. Rescoring against the refit and growing the consensus costs a couple of extra solves and recovered the three inliers my test knew were missing.

A guard clause can break the property it is guarding. Dividing by std + 1e-8 looks like harmless defensive code and it quietly destroyed the gain invariance that the normalisation exists to provide, because an absolute epsilon means one thing to a bright patch and something else to a dim one. The mosaics came out fine, so looking at them would never have found it. A test asserting the invariance exactly found it on the first run, and exactness was the whole point of that assertion.
